What changed this month

September 2026
  • RBA

    The RBA raised the cash rate 25 basis points to 4.60% on 29 September in a unanimous decision, its fourth increase of 2026 and the highest setting since late 2011. The Board said some of the upside risks to inflation were materialising, citing further disruptions to global oil supply, growth and inflation running above its expectations, and partial pass through of fuel costs to other prices. It repeated that it will raise the cash rate further if needed. The next decision is on 3 November, after the September quarter CPI on 28 October.

  • Inflation

    Headline CPI rose to 4.0% in the year to August from 3.5% in July, led by housing at 5.7% and transport at 5.6%, with new dwelling prices up 5.4% as builders passed on materials and labour costs. Trimmed mean held at 3.6%, which the ABS describes as a third consecutive month at that rate, with automotive fuel and electricity excluded and accounting for most of the gap to headline. EY estimates six month annualised trimmed mean at 3.9%, above the twelve month rate. Melbourne Institute inflation expectations held at 4.9% in August.

  • Central banks

    Four other central banks on this page moved inside four weeks. The RBNZ raised to 2.75% on 2 September, the ECB raised the deposit rate to 2.50% on 10 September, the Federal Reserve lifted its target range to 3.75% to 4.00% on 16 September on a 12 to 0 vote, its first increase since 2023, and the Bank of Japan moved to 1.25% on 18 September, carried 7 to 2. The Bank of England held at 3.75% on 17 September with Greene, Mann and Pill voting for 4.00%, and the Federal Reserve's projections have 16 of 18 participants expecting at least one further rise this year.

  • Bond yields

    The United States 10 year closed September at 5.28%, up 53 basis points for the month and its highest since 2007, with the 30 year reaching 5.56%, its highest since 2004. The Australian 10 year reached 5.40% on 15 September, the highest since May 2011, and closed the month at 5.35%, up 26 basis points. Japanese 10 year yields reached a thirty year high and German 10 year yields their highest since 2009. The Bloomberg AusBond Composite returned minus 0.9% and the Global Aggregate hedged minus 1.6%. ClearBridge attributes most of the United States move since late February to real yields, up 50 basis points against 15 for inflation expectations and 17 for the term premium.

  • Oil and the Gulf

    Strikes between the United States and Iran resumed around the Strait of Hormuz in the first week of September, and damage to three pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East to West pipeline took Brent to a four month high near US$106 on 15 September. Brent closed the month at US$103.5, up 14.4% for September and 70.1% for the calendar year. Goldman Sachs lifted its December 2026 Brent forecast by US$5 to US$85. The EIA assumes Middle East export constraints persist through the end of the year, with regional output below prewar averages until the June quarter of 2027.

  • Jobs and business

    Unemployment rose to 4.6% in August, the highest since November 2021, as employment rose 39,500 and participation lifted 0.2 points to 67.1%, so more people entered the labour force than found work. The ABS has changed its supplementary survey method from the August data and recommends the trend series, which also sits at 4.6%. NAB business conditions fell 5 points to minus 1 in August, the first negative reading in six years, with profitability down 10 points to minus 9. Westpac Melbourne Institute consumer sentiment fell 5.2% to 84.4 in September.

  • Housing

    Cotality's national index fell 1.1% in September, a sixth consecutive monthly decline, leaving values 5.2% below the March peak and unchanged over twelve months. Brisbane fell 1.5% and Sydney 1.4%, with Sydney now 8.6% below its February peak, and 97% of capital city suburbs fell over the September quarter. Sales over the past three months are tracking 19.1% below a year earlier. Cotality's Tim Lawless expects values to keep falling into 2027.

  • Private credit

    Bathla Group entered voluntary administration on 25 August with about $3.4 billion of known debt, including $3.08 billion owed to secured lenders across roughly 40 private credit funds, and the NSW Supreme Court has extended the convening period to 13 September 2027. Metrics Credit Partners, which manages about $40 billion, suspended three ASX listed funds on 28 September after its auditor KPMG took different positions on valuation inputs, cutting net tangible assets by between 1.99% and 12.16%, and on 30 September suspended redemptions from the underlying wholesale funds. ASIC Chair Sarah Court has described Bathla as a troubling development for the sector, with the regulator's concerns centred on property development and construction lending.

  • Australian equities

    The ASX 200 Accumulation Index returned minus 2.4% in September to close at 8,789, ending five consecutive monthly gains, and is up 3.7% for the calendar year. Information technology fell 10.6% and materials 6.0% as gold and iron ore fell, with BHP alone taking 76 basis points off the index, while health care (+4.4%) and energy (+0.6%) were the only sectors higher. The forward multiple eased to 17.1 times while FY27 consensus earnings growth was cut to 7.2%, and Morgan Stanley sees risk to earnings on the downside into AGM season. Small caps returned minus 2.3% and A-REITs minus 1.9%.

  • Global equities

    The MSCI All Countries World Index returned 2.0% in Australian dollars as the currency fell 3.1% against the US dollar to 0.6947, which turned mostly negative local markets into positive unhedged returns. In local currency the S&P 500 returned minus 0.3%, the DAX minus 4.0% and the STOXX 600 minus 2.5%, while the Nikkei added 1.3%. MSCI World ex Australia returned 1.6% unhedged against minus 0.5% hedged.

Overall signal: expansion. Eleven of twelve variables green with job sentiment the sole recession reading, and no indicator changes in September. ClearBridge frames the Federal Reserve's 16 September rise as the start of a cycle entered from strength, with consumer credit growing at the weakest non-recessionary pace on record and most borrowing costs still below their 2023 peaks. The Bankrate 30 year fixed mortgage rate is 7.3% against 8.1% in 2023, investment grade corporate bonds yield about 6.0% against 6.4%, and high yield bonds 8.4% against 9.5%. Federal funds futures price roughly 115 basis points of cumulative hikes over just over a year, a steeper path than the Federal Reserve's own projections. ClearBridge notes that would still be the smallest tightening cycle in modern history, against 425 basis points in 2004 to 2006 and 525 basis points in 2022 to 2023. Consensus has S&P 500 earnings per share growing 18.7% over the next twelve months.

ClearBridge published this reading on 30 September with data as at that date, so it reflects the Federal Reserve's 16 September rise and the 10 year Treasury yield's close of 5.28%. ClearBridge notes United States recessions have on average begun more than a year after the Federal Reserve finished hiking. It names an inverted yield curve, widening credit spreads and a rollover in leading indicators of employment and activity as the signs that tighter policy is biting, and reports none of them at present. Betashares reports global high yield spreads widened about 0.5 percentage points in September, while ClearBridge's credit spreads indicator, which tracks United States spreads, remains green.

Risk register

ElevatedBond yields and the discount rate

The United States 10 year closed September at 5.28%, its highest since 2007, and the Australian 10 year at 5.35% after peaking at 5.40% on 15 September, with Japanese and German yields at multi-decade highs. Morgan Stanley puts the ASX 200 forward earnings yield only 0.6 percentage points above the bond, while FY27 consensus earnings growth has been cut to 7.2% and three month revisions are negative.

ElevatedEnergy and the Strait of Hormuz

Brent finished September at US$103.5, up 14.4% for the month and 70.1% for the calendar year, after reaching US$106 on 15 September when three pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East to West pipeline were damaged. The EIA assumes export constraints persist through the end of the year. Fuel and electricity carried Australian headline CPI to 4.0% in August.

ElevatedAustralian housing correction

Values have fallen for six consecutive months and sit 5.2% below the March peak, with 97% of capital city suburbs down over the September quarter and sales 19.1% below a year earlier. Cotality expects falls to continue into 2027, and the 29 September increase adds to repayment pressure on variable rate borrowers.

ElevatedPrivate credit and property lending

Bathla's administration, with about $3.4 billion of known debt across roughly 40 lenders, has been followed by redemption limits at several property credit funds and, on 30 September, the suspension of redemptions from Metrics wholesale funds managing more than $11 billion. ASIC's concerns are centred on development and construction lending, where falling dwelling values and higher rates test valuations and refinancing.

WatchBusiness margins and the labour market

NAB profitability fell to minus 9 in August, a post-pandemic low, with purchase costs at 2.3% in quarterly terms against output prices at 0.8%, and unemployment rose to 4.6% in the same month. NAB notes profitability tends to lead employment. The September labour force release is on 15 October.

UpsideThe technology cycle running longer

Consensus has S&P 500 earnings per share growing 18.7% over the next twelve months, per ClearBridge, and Betashares at the end of September had global forward earnings growing 17% to the end of 2027 against 8% for Australia. The MSCI All Countries World Index returned 2.0% in Australian dollars in September while the ASX 200 Accumulation Index returned minus 2.4%.

Structural: the productivity gap

Ausbil, Jim Chronis · 7 May 2026 · updated twice yearly

Australian productivity has been flat at 0% since 2022 while United States productivity has run at 2% or better for five years, a gap Chronis illustrated as the difference between travelling at 60 and 100 kilometres an hour. Unit labour costs have grown around 5% annually over the past two years against 2% in the United States, which makes the inflation target structurally harder for the RBA to reach and sustains restrictive policy independently of where the cycle sits.

Government spending sits at roughly 28% of GDP with investment at only 5%, maintaining the existing capital stock rather than adding productive capacity, while consumption including the NDIS continues to grow and crowd it out. Ausbil expects GDP to slow from 2.3% to around 1.8% and remain below trend until a private sector led pickup in the second half of 2027, which Chronis characterises as a moderation rather than a contraction. The 2025-26 financial year came in at 2.4%, ahead of that path, and this section is next due for refresh in November.
