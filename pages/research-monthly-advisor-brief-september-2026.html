Key market events in September
7 to 15 September
Conflict around Hormuz and a Saudi pipeline outage take Brent above US$100

Strikes between the United States and Iran resumed around the Strait of Hormuz, and damage to three pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East to West pipeline took Brent to a four month high near US$106 on 15 September. Oil finished the month around US$103, up 14.4% for September and 72.0% for the calendar year, and fuel and electricity lifted Australian headline CPI to 4.0% in August.

10 to 29 September
Four central banks raise rates, including the RBA to 4.60%

The ECB raised to 2.50% on 10 September, the Federal Reserve to 3.75% to 4.00% on 16 September, its first increase since 2023, and the Bank of Japan to 1.25% on 18 September. The RBA followed on 29 September with a unanimous 25 basis point increase to 4.60%, its fourth of the year, saying some upside risks to inflation were materialising.

15 to 28 September
Long bond yields reach multi-decade highs

The Australian 10 year yield reached 5.40% on 15 September, the highest since May 2011, and finished the month at 5.35%. The United States 10 year closed September at 5.28%, up 53 basis points in the month and its highest since 2007. The Bloomberg AusBond Composite returned negative 0.9% and information technology shares fell 10.6%, the two areas most exposed to higher discount rates.

28 to 30 September
Metrics Credit Partners suspends listed trusts and wholesale fund redemptions

Metrics suspended its three ASX listed trusts on 28 September and redemptions from its underlying wholesale funds on 30 September. Metrics is not on the Vantage approved list, and our private credit view is set out at the end of this brief.

What moved markets in September

September was a month in which rates did the damage. Brent returned above US$100 on renewed conflict around the Strait of Hormuz, four of the major central banks we track raised rates, and long bond yields finished the month at 5.35% in Australia and 5.28% in the United States, up 26 and 53 basis points respectively. The S&P/ASX 200 Accumulation Index returned negative 2.4% to close at 8,789, ending five consecutive monthly gains, with information technology down 10.6% and materials down 6.0% as gold fell 6.3% and iron ore 5.9%. Fixed income

Why long bond yields have risen

Long bond yields reached their highest levels in fifteen years in Australia and nineteen years in the United States in September. The rise matters for every part of a diversified portfolio because the 10 year yield sets the discount rate for all other assets.

Where yields are

  • The Australian 10 year reached 5.40% on 15 September, the highest since May 2011, and finished the month at 5.35%, up 26 basis points in September and from 4.64% at the end of February.
  • The United States 10 year closed September at 5.28%, up 53 basis points in the month and its highest since 2007, with the 30 year reaching 5.56%. Japanese, UK, French and German yields also reached multi-year highs.

What is driving them

  • Inflation and the policy path. Brent is up 72% this year and Australian headline CPI is back to 4.0%. United States futures have moved from pricing several rate cuts in February to pricing further increases, and the RBA, Federal Reserve, ECB and Bank of Japan all raised rates in September.
  • Real yields more than inflation fears. ClearBridge's decomposition attributes most of the United States rise since late February to real yields, and the ten year breakeven inflation rate was 2.33% on 18 September. Viola Private Wealth, using the Federal Reserve's Kim-Wright model over March to September, puts about half the rise down to term premium, so estimates differ on how much is compensation for uncertainty.
  • Supply. Treasury raised its September quarter borrowing estimate to US$739 billion, US$68 billion above May, while technology companies funding AI infrastructure are issuing record amounts of corporate debt that competes for the same buyers.

What it means for portfolios

  • Bond prices fall when yields rise. The AusBond Composite returned negative 0.9% in September and the Vantage Fixed Interest portfolio negative 0.45%, with the shorter duration holdings positive.
  • Real assets carry the most rate sensitivity. Listed infrastructure and property discount long-dated cash flows, which is why the Property and Infrastructure portfolio returned negative 4.99% in the month.
  • The equity cushion is thin. The ASX 200 trades on 17.1 times forward earnings, a forward earnings yield of about 5.8%, which Morgan Stanley puts only 0.6 percentage points above the 10 year bond. The S&P 500's 5.6% earnings yield on 2027 forecasts sits about 0.3 points above United States Treasuries. With FY27 consensus earnings growth for the ASX 200 cut to 7.2%, equities need that growth delivered to justify current prices.

Outlook

  • Better income from defensive assets. Starting yields above 5% are the best for Australian bonds since 2011 and give more protection against further price falls.
  • Duration of high rates is the key risk. Companies and households are still partly insulated by borrowing taken out at lower rates, and that protection fades the longer yields stay near current levels and more debt is refinanced.
  • More modest returns ahead. With the cash rate at 4.60% and long bonds above 5%, we anticipate returns from diversified portfolios over the coming years will be lower and more variable than the strong results of the past twelve months.

Australian private credit

Press coverage of Australian private credit increased through September, driven by the administration of Bathla Group and the suspension of redemptions at Metrics Credit Partners. Below is what has happened, where our exposure sits and our view on each manager we use.

What has happened

  • Bathla Group. The Sydney developer entered voluntary administration on 25 August owing about $3.4 billion, including $3.08 billion to secured lenders across roughly 40 private credit funds. Centuria Bass and CVS Lane have limited redemptions, and MA Financial capped its separate MA Secured Real Estate Income Fund at 1% a month from 31 August.
  • ASIC. ASIC Chair Sarah Court described Bathla as a troubling development for the sector, and the regulator's concerns are centred on property development and construction lending.
  • Metrics. On 28 September Metrics suspended its three ASX listed trusts, and on 30 September it suspended redemptions from the underlying wholesale funds. Metrics is not on the Vantage approved list.

Where our exposure sits

  • No to very limited construction exposure. Vantage deliberately avoids property development and construction lending in private credit, the segment at the centre of ASIC's concerns.
  • Approved list, not managed accounts. The private credit funds are approved list options for client portfolios and are not held within the Vantage managed account portfolios.
  • Close contact with managers. The investment team remains in close contact with each manager we use and will update advisers as needed.

Our view on each manager

Keyview Flagship Fund
  • Comfortable with the Bathla exposure. Keyview has been a focus for the team as it held four loans connected to Bathla. All four are secured over completed or near-completed land subdivision projects.
  • Ring-fenced from the administration. The loans sit in special purpose vehicles that are bankruptcy remote from Bathla's administration, so repayment depends on lot settlements rather than the group's survival.
  • Expected to repay in full. Keyview is very confident the loans will be repaid in full and on time, and it has no redemption restrictions in place. We will report on repayment as the loans settle.
Realm Strategic Income Fund
  • Positive on the strategy. The portfolio is well diversified and has strong risk protections built into its structure.
  • Deep loss buffer. Portfolio modelling shows Australian house prices would need to fall more than 40% before the portfolio realises a loss.
  • Outside the sectors of concern. With no direct property construction or software exposure, Realm is not directly exposed to the current private credit sectors of concern.
Revolution Wholesale Private Debt Fund II
  • Conservative side of private credit. The fund lends senior secured to corporates, asset-backed securities and real estate debt, and excludes construction and development lending.
  • Software exposure is in mission critical names. Revolution has some software exposure, but in businesses whose products are mission critical to their customers.
  • Highly experienced team. The fund is led by a highly experienced investment team.
MA Priority Income Fund
  • Structural protection. MA holds a 10% first loss position beneath investors, so MA's capital absorbs losses before investors do.
  • Diversified lending. The fund lends mainly against asset-backed and corporate loans rather than development projects.
  • Separate from the capped fund. The 1% redemption limit applies to MA's separate real estate credit fund, which we do not use.

Private credit earns a place in client portfolios only where we can see the underlying loans, understand the structure and judge the manager's discipline in deploying capital, and we will keep applying that standard to every manager on the approved list as lending conditions tighten.
